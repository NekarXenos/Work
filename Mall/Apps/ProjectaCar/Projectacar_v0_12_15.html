How scale worksTL;DRRim Ø sets the model's metric scale. A known wheelbase (hub centre to hub centre) can supply it instead. The rim size is the 17 in 225/45R17. Nothing changes until you press Apply.
The rim diameter sets the metric scale for the whole model, and it is the one number a photograph cannot reveal. A wheelbase can supply it instead: the model is exactly proportional to the rim diameter, so a known wheelbase divides the right rim size straight out, and it is published for almost every car when the wheel size is not. Nothing here changes the model until you press Apply.

Wheelbase is measured from the centre of the front hub to the centre of the rear hub, on one side, with the wheels pointed straight ahead. A tape along the ground from the middle of one wheel to the middle of the other is close enough — a millimetre or two is a hundredth of a percent here.

Rim Ø is the bare metal, bead to bead, and does not include the rubber. Read it off the tyre sidewall rather than measuring: in 225/45R17 the 17 is the rim diameter in inches, 225 is the section width in millimetres and 45 is the sidewall height as a percentage of that width. The tyre Ø chip works the outside diameter out from those three numbers so you can compare it against the circle you traced, but it never overwrites it.

How auto-solve worksTL;DRMoves your points as little as possible to make the geometry consistent. Locked items never move. Applies only on Apply, and never if a reading got worse. Consistent is not the same as right: a large focal-per-px means spread your guides wider.
Auto-solve moves your clicked points as little as it can while making the geometry consistent. A locked element is removed from the solve outright, so it cannot move at all. Nothing is applied until you press Apply, and nothing is applied that made any of the three readings worse.

It makes the geometry consistent, which is not the same as making it right. A photograph carries no truth to check against, so only consistency is knowable from your guides. Watch focal per px: it is how many pixels of focal length ride on one pixel of clicking. When it is large, the rim points are too close together for the focal length to mean much, and the answer is a wider-spread guide rather than a longer solve.
How the camera solve worksTL;DRThe four rim points form a rectangle whose two vanishing points give the focal length. Box vs axle is the pixel disagreement check. Circularity reads 1.000 until a depth-line vanishing point takes over.
The six points solve the camera. The four rim points bound a wheelbase × diameter rectangle standing in the wheel plane: its long edges converge at the longitudinal vanishing point, the two rim chords at the vertical one, and two orthogonal vanishing points give the focal length outright. A steered front wheel is fine: steering turns the wheel about a vertical axis and cannot change its height, so the two points barely move.

Box vs axle is the check. The long edges of the box are longitudinal, and so is the line through the two axle centres — an axle recessed into the wheel sits on a parallel world line, and parallel lines share a vanishing point, so the recess costs nothing. The two therefore have to agree, and this is the pixel distance by which they do not. The solve uses the point pulled onto the axle line.

Circularity reads 1.000 while the box is in charge, because a disc built from the box is a circle by construction. It becomes a real measurement again only when a depth-line vanishing point is applied over it.

Ground & symmetry

tyre Ø — deflection — flat spot — nearer wheel — camera height — track — rear width — skew — far flat spot — 3d state — mirror offset — pairs 0 lateral vp — harmonic vs metric — section — front tyre Ø — rake — front steer —
How ground & track workTL;DRThe wheel-bottom line's contact corner gives tyre radius, ride height and flat spot. Draw it at the rear wheel, outer face to outer face (land on the far tyre's bright outboard edge). Harmonic vs metric should agree.
Nothing about the tyre is dragged. The end of the wheel-bottom line nearest the rim is the corner of the contact patch in the rim's own plane, so back-projecting that one pixel onto the plane already solved gives everything: the distance from the rim centre is the tyre radius, its vertical part is the ride height, and its longitudinal part is half the flat spot. A loaded tyre does not touch down below its axle, and it no longer has to pretend to — the contact corner is measured where it actually is, nearer the camera, and the flat spot falls out as the chord the road cuts across the tyre.

Draw the line at the rear wheel. The front one can be steered, which takes it out of the rear's plane; the front tyre gets its radius by dropping its own axle onto the solved road instead.

The line runs outer face to outer face: the near tyre's outboard side, which is the rim plane, across to the far tyre's outboard side. That far face is the mirror of where you started, and it is not the far tyre's visible inner edge — that edge lies one section width closer to you, and taking it for the outer face makes the track come out one section width short. Once the tyres are extruded the far wheel draws both edges, the outboard one bright: the far end of your line should sit on the bright one. Both are mirror images of each other, so their midpoint lands on the mirror plane whatever the tyre width — which is why track (centre-plane to centre-plane) and rear width (what you clicked) are reported as two different numbers rather than one.

Harmonic vs metric is the cross-check. The harmonic conjugate finds a symmetric pair's midpoint from the lateral vanishing point alone; the metric route finds it from the full solve. They should agree. Where they don't, the pixels tell you by how much.

Wheel arches

clearance — lift — skirt height — roll axis height — roll rear/front — sideskirt inset — arch depth rear/front — corner bend reach — flange extrusion — flare rear/front — flare lift — flare tilt — flare depth rear/front —
Arch handles explainedTL;DRDrag out from a tyre to trace the arch; the skirt line sets its bottom. Dot = fillet, diamond = chamfer. Blue = roll, cyan = sideskirt inset, green = flange, magenta = depth, yellow = bend reach.
Drag outward from a tyre to trace its half circle, in the rim's own plane. Drag the vertical centre-line up or down to lift the whole arch. Drag the horizontal skirt line — it starts level with the rim centre — down to where the skirting or bumper actually ends: where it crosses the half circle, the circle is cut off there and that crossing is the bottom corner; where it falls short of the circle, the circle's own ends are run straight down to meet it. Straighten sides swaps the round cap for flat tangent lines — up from each bottom corner, along the side tangents, across a flat top — each run only as far as the next. Each side tangent has its own handle, right where it touches the circle; drag it around the circle to change that side's angle (45° until moved). The two corners this leaves each get TWO small handles of their own, a dot and a diamond sitting together at the untouched corner: drag the dot inward to round the corner off (a fillet, reaching the original half circle at the far end of the drag), drag the diamond inward to cut it flat instead (a chamfer, reaching the flat chord between its neighbours at the far end). Both work on the same corner at once — chamfer it first and then fillet what's left to smooth the two new corners the cut made, or use either alone. Mirrored fore-aft by default — untick to shape the front and back of one arch differently.

A real arch is not flat: it covers the top of the wheel and tucks in near the sill at the bottom. The small blue dot is the roll axis — one shared height, drag either wheel's dot to move it — and it marks the one height where the arch stays exactly on the rim's own plane; choose it to match where the bodywork actually meets the rim. The hollow blue dot at the arch's own top is the roll handle: drag it outward, away from the tyre, and the whole arch — circular or straightened, corners and all — rotates open around the axis like a hinged flap, tucked in at the axis and flared out above it. Set independently per wheel, front and rear often want different amounts.

The cyan dot below the arches is the sideskirt handle, at the midpoint of a line run between the two wheels' facing bottom corners. Drag it inward, toward the car's own centreline, and the sideskirt itself stays perfectly straight and slides in as one rigid piece, exactly like the real part it represents — it is the ARCH that gives instead: each arch's own bottom corner bends inward to follow, fillet-style, smooth and tangent where it meets the now-shifted sideskirt rather than kinking into it, since most sideskirts sit a little tucked in compared to the arches above them. The same rigid inward slide, and the same bending corner, mirrors onto the outer bottom corners as short matching stubs pointed at where the front and rear bumpers will be.

The green dot just clear of each arch's own top is the flange handle: drag it further out, up the same plane the arch is already rolled to, and the line fans outward into a real panel — a flat trim ring of constant width, growing off the wheel opening rather than standing up out of it, one shared width for both wheels. Its bottom corners taper shut using the exact same bend the plain arch already uses to meet the sideskirt, so the flange narrows to nothing right at the seam rather than ending in a floating edge. Orbit to see it textured: the traced edge keeps its own photograph position and the fanned-out edge borrows it, the same way the tyre's own hidden faces do; the far side, once shown, borrows in turn from the near flange of its own axle.

Roll tilts the arch; it never moves it. The bright magenta dot inside each arch, level with the centre-line handle and a little forward of it, is the depth handle: drag it sideways and the whole arch — size, roll, corners and all — slides bodily in or out along the depth plane, perpendicular to the rim. Nothing about its shape changes; it simply stands somewhere else. That matters because the plane you traced is the wheel's own OUTBOARD face, and the bodywork around the opening almost never sits exactly on it — most arches stand a little proud of the rim, some tuck behind it. It reads only that one direction, so dragging it up and down does nothing; lifting the arch stays the cyan centre-line handle's job. A short dotted tail shows how far it has been moved from the rim plane it was traced on. Everything welded to the arch follows it: the sideskirt's own base edge, the flange, the flare and the sill surface all re-solve against the arch's new position. Linked front to rear by the same checkbox that links fillet, chamfer and roll.

How far up the arch that bend reaches is its own handle now. The small hollow yellow dot sitting on each arch, a short way up from the bottom corner nearest the sideskirt, marks exactly where the corner starts turning in — below it the arch is bending toward the skirt, above it the arch is untouched, and a dotted tail runs down to the corner so the bending stretch is the stretch you can see. Drag the dot up the arch to start the bend higher, so the corner sweeps in over a long, lazy reach; drag it back down toward the corner to keep the arch true almost all the way and turn in sharply at the last moment. Until you touch it the reach is worked out from the inset alone, which is a fair guess but only a guess — where a real arch stops following the wheel is a fact about that car's panel, not about how far its sill happens to be tucked. One shared amount, like the sideskirt inset itself: it shapes all four bottom corners at once, and the flange and the flare taper shut on the same reach, so the whole seam stays one piece. It is grabbable on step 8 as well, since that is where the skirt is still being slid.

Sideskirt panel

rise — lean — top edge inset — bottom span — top span — trim rear/front —
Sideskirt handles explainedTL;DRThe toggle picks the drag mode: follow the arch curve, or vertical + in/out. Hollow end dots trim the top into a trapezium. Green dots grow and shape a second flange (flare); magenta sets its depth.
This panel drags in two different ways — the toggle above switches between them, and switching hands the panel off exactly where it currently sits, with no jump.

Follow arch curve (the default): the skirt line is only a line until you drag its one bright dot upward. Rather than moving in a straight line, the dot slides along a virtual surface built from each wheel arch's own curve — the very shape the arch already bends into right where it meets this line — continued on up along the arch and joined between the rear wheel and the front one. Drag it further and the panel does not just rise, it leans the way the fender does, because that lean is built into the curve itself rather than dragged in as a separate move. The drag stops once it has climbed about one wheel radius of curve, past which a sideskirt stops making sense.

Vertical + in/out: the classic pair. The bright dot raises the panel straight up, nothing else. Just beside it sits a second, purple dot, the lean handle: drag it sideways — inward to tuck the sill under the body, outward to flare it — and it alone moves the top edge that way. Each reads only the one direction it owns, so raising the sill never shifts its lean and tucking it never changes its height. The top edge is a rigid parallel copy of the bottom one in this mode, so it runs to the same vanishing point the bottom edge does — and so does a real door-bottom seam, which is a straight line down the side of the car like any other, which is what makes aiming at one worth doing. If the drawn edge sits on the seam in the middle of the car but drifts away at one end, no amount of dragging will fix it: the drift is telling you about the camera solve, or about where one arch's bottom corner was put, and it is worth going back for rather than dragged away.

Once the panel is standing, in either mode, two hollow dots appear at the ends of the top edge. Drag either along the edge to shorten the top while the bottom stays full length, so that end slopes inward into the wheel arch — the panel becomes a trapezium, which is how a sill really does tuck into a wheel opening. The two can never cross.

The moment an end is trimmed — and that wheel's arch flange has been extruded — a green dot appears up past the flange's own top. Drag it to grow a second flange off the first one's outer edge: it fans out wider still, but rotates back to vertical and pulls in until it lies flat in the vertical plane that carries the trimmed sill top. Its near end closes to a sharp corner right on that trimmed top end; its far end fillets shut against the sideskirt exactly where the plain arch and the first flange already do. It only builds on the side whose sill end is trimmed — the front handle needs the front end shortened, the rear the rear.

That green dot sets how far the flare fans out, and it is one of four. A second green dot sits on the flare's own top edge, a little way aft: drag it to lift the whole outer edge up or down, exactly the way the arch's own centre-line handle lifts the arch. Because the flare's inner edge is welded to the first flange and cannot move with it, lifting does not slide the panel — it changes the angle the flare stands at, from pointing up and proud to dropping down and under. Forward of the fan-out dot are two blue ones, the same pair the arch already has: the small solid dot is the tilt axis, the height the flare pivots about, and the hollow one above it is the tilt itself. Drag the hollow dot toward you and the flare opens outward; push it the other way and it tucks in deeper, under the body — some cars want one, some the other.

The flare lands, by default, flat in the vertical plane that carries the trimmed sill top — the one plane that passes exactly through the flare's own bottom corner, which is why that corner closes without a seam. But where the plane sits is a fact about the sill, not about the flare: raise or lean the sill and the flare goes with it. The magenta dot, a little further aft again past the lift handle, is the flare's own depth handle — the same move the wheel arch has on step 7, one plane over. Drag it sideways and the flare's outer edge slides in or out along the depth plane, off the sill's plane and onto its own. It reads only that one direction, so dragging it up and down does nothing; raising the flare stays the lift handle's job, and a dotted tail shows how far it has been moved. Like lift and tilt it moves the outer edge alone: the inner edge is welded to the first flange and the two ends are welded to the sill-top corner and the sideskirt, so what a depth drag changes is how far the panel leans out, not where it is attached. All four of these are per wheel, and the checkbox at the top of this panel keeps the front and rear matched unless you untick it; the tilt axis is a single shared height either way, like the arch's own.

Orbit to see it textured. Unlike the arch flange, whose fanned-out edge is metal the camera never saw and has to borrow its pixels, both edges of a sill are in the photograph — the lower lip and the door seam — which is the only reason you could aim at one. So the near panel wears its own pixels, sampled where it truly projects. Only the far panel, which was never photographed, borrows from the near one across the mirror plane.

Vertical doorline

segment 1 — shoulder fwd/aft — segment 2 — door-top fwd/aft —
Doorline handles explainedTL;DRDrag the purple dots up from the sill to the shoulder line, then to the door top. Each half-line tip has reach (dot), height (hollow) and depth (diamond). Click the line to add a bend; drag it onto a neighbour to remove.
One solid vertical line, traced off the sill's own top edge in two segments. Drag the first purple dot along the sill to where the doorline meets it. Then drag the second dot onto the photo where the doorline crosses the shoulder line — or the base of the side window, whichever line runs back from the corner where the hood meets the windscreen. That draws the lower stretch of the doorline solid.

Now the shoulder line has two half-lines, one ahead of the doorline and one behind, and each is independent. On each you get a small cluster of three handles at its tip: the filled dot reaches it fore or aft (this is what opens the panel — the gap down to the end of the sideskirt top fills with a quad); once it has length, a hollow dot just above raises or lowers that tip, and a diamond just inboard slides it in or out on the depth plane. The inboard end stays welded to the doorline, so lifting or insetting a tip angles that half-line and skews its panel rather than detaching it.

Repeat for the second segment: drag its point up to where the doorline meets the top of the door, then shape its own two half-lines the same way — a second pair of panels stacks on the first, shoulder line up to door-top line.

Real B-pillars are rarely dead straight. Click anywhere on the doorline to drop a bend point there, then drag it onto the photographed edge — the panels on both sides fold to follow, subdividing to match. A bend belongs to whichever segment its height puts it in, so the order sorts itself out no matter how you drag. To remove one, drag it back onto the base, shoulder or top dot.

Every bend also grows its own fore and aft cross-line, drawn solid, each with the same three handles as the shoulder line — a filled dot to lengthen it fore or aft, a hollow dot to raise or lower its tip, a diamond to slide it in or out on the depth plane. They start matching the straight door width and the panel edge runs through them, so you can taper or curve the fore/aft faces of the door section by section. The sill’s own top edge subdivides the same way, and the sideskirt beneath it splits vertically at the doorline and at every crease that reaches it — each of those rungs carrying its own node on the skirt’s bottom edge as well, draggable on its own for a skirt that is not a straight band. Orbit to see all the panels textured from the photo, mirrored to the far side of the car.

Depth + mirror profile

ribs 0 nodes 0 pending — widest line not set yet height span —
How the profile worksTL;DRDraw top, widest and bottom depth lines; they chain down the mirror plane into the body skin. Dotted = as drawn, solid = snapped to the vanishing point; Accept commits. Click the chain to add nodes, double-click a line to subdivide.
A depth line is a left-right symmetric pair, so its 3d midpoint lands on the mirror plane and the bar itself is purely lateral. Two numbers describe it: a point on that plane and a half width. Draw a top line across the windscreen, a bottom line across the bumper, and keep step 4's widest line between them. If the widest point of the body is the top or the bottom, convert it and work with two.

Their centres join into one chain down the mirror plane and their outer ends into a second chain beside it; the skin between the two is the body, mirrored across the car.

Dotted is what you drew, kept exactly as drawn. Solid is the same line swung onto the current lateral vanishing point: the near end is pinned, because that is the one the photograph shows clearly, and the far end — the outboard face of the far side, which it does not — is dropped onto the ray. Nothing re-solves while a line is pending: the vanishing point holds still. Accept moves the line onto the point; Auto-solve is the one action that instead moves the point onto the line, behind its usual Apply / Cancel.

Click anywhere on the centre chain to drop a node. It moves freely in the mirror plane, and its own rib lengthens or shortens from the dot at its outer end. Drag a node onto a neighbour to remove it. Ribs order themselves by height, so a node dragged above the top line simply becomes the new top of the chain.

Double-click anywhere on a depth line to subdivide the whole profile at once: every other depth line narrows to the same percentage of its own width as the point you clicked. Each line's original width does not disappear — it stays behind as a dimmed, dashed companion, joined to the new narrower line by a solid edge, and a real panel is ruled between the two the same way the main skin is. The companion has its own dot at its centre, draggable in the mirror plane just like a node's, and its own width dot, so the two rails can be pulled apart into a real step or crease rather than sitting on top of each other. Double-click again to narrow further; the companion, once made, only moves if you drag it.

Bumper blend

bumper top not set yet bumper ribs 0 runs on to — far end depth — blend groups —
How the bumper blend worksTL;DRDrag the orange dot on the centre chain to the bumper top; the sideskirt runs on and curves into it. Pair blend nodes: click the single side first, then its partners, Enter per group, Esc to cancel.
Step 10 leaves a chain of depth lines running down the mirror plane, and step 8 leaves a sideskirt that stops dead at each wheel arch. This is where the two meet.

First say where the top of the bumper is: click the centre chain and drag the orange dot to the height the bumper actually starts. Every depth line at or below it is a bumper line from then on, and which end of the car that is — front or rear — is worked out from where those lines sit, not asked for. Nothing here exists until that dot is placed.

The sideskirt then runs on past the arch toward it, on exactly the settings the sideskirt itself is standing on — same mode, same rise, same lean — so the two read as one band the wheel arch happens to interrupt. It stops halfway to whichever bumper line sits closest to the wheel, and its far end carries two handles: the orange one lengthens or shortens the run, and the magenta one pushes that end in or out on the depth plane — the same move the arch and the flare each have, one plane over. The arch end never moves; it is a weld.

From there it blends into the bumper in a curve, leaving the sill along the sill's own direction and arriving at the bumper along the bumper's own, so the corner reads as bodywork rather than a cut. Which node goes to which is yours to say. Press Pair blend nodes, then for each group click the side that has one node first and then click its partners on the other side; Enter confirms that group and moves to the next. There are usually more nodes on the bumper than on the skirt, which is exactly why they go in groups. Esc clears the group you are building, and a second Esc leaves without changing anything. Finish with a node still unpicked and it simply joins the group vertically closest to it — the same rule that pairs everything by height before you touch it, so there is never a hole in the surface.

What A / B / C meanTL;DRRims and depth lines imply different lateral vanishing points. B (keep rims, default) trusts the firmer ellipse; A trusts the lines; C blends between the two.
Two independent readings of the lateral vanishing point: one the rim ellipses imply, one fitted to the depth lines you drew. The fans from the photo\u2019s corners show where each one sits \u2014 pink from the rims, violet from the lines, cyan for the one in use.

B is the default because an ellipse traced over hundreds of pixels is firmer evidence than two clicked endpoints \u2014 in that mode each line is also drawn toward where the rims say it should point, so you can pull it onto the mark. A takes the lines at their word and leaves the rims to be re-traced. C meets them in between, bounded by the two so it can never wander outside what either measurement supports.

Views

About the viewsTL;DRWireframes redraw once depth is solved. The orbit cell keeps its angle and zoom; the main view always snaps back to the photo.
The five wireframe cells redraw the instant any depth is solved — that is what they are for. Until then your tracing sits flat on the drawing plane, which is exactly where an unknown depth belongs. The orbit cell keeps whatever angle and zoom you leave it at; the main view always snaps back to the photograph.
